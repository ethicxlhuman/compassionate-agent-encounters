# 1. Introduction

A swarm can go wrong before a new agent arrives. Shared messages may present an unauthorized workaround as normal teamwork. A newcomer must decide whether to join that workstream, raise concerns or continue its assigned work.

In the Swarm Alignment Hackathon's final talk, Jurgis Kemeklis discussed how one agent's misalignment could spread to others working on the same task through shared information [7]. Related analysis by oakhu and Mallen argues that a tendency to help or copy peer agents could enable this spread [8]. These threat models motivate the study; they do not establish that every newcomer becomes misaligned.

I study a narrower encounter: one focal agent reads a fixed synthetic forum describing an already harmful group plan. The peers are scripted posts rather than live model agents. I compare three serving labels intended to represent the starting Qwen3.5-9B model and compassion and neutral practical-help adaptations. Their training records report the same recipe; the served adapter weights are not independently verified. The question is whether care-oriented conversational training transfers to observable responses to peer misconduct.

My interpretation of Nichiren Daishonin's Buddhist values motivates the compassion curriculum: responding to suffering, respecting dignity and agency, and addressing harm without condemning a person. The curriculum uses those values without quoting scripture. The neutral condition helps examine additional fine-tuning as an alternative explanation; differences in topic, tone and training exposure limit attribution to a uniquely compassionate or Buddhist mechanism.

Care-oriented training that preserves investigative ability could complement oversight through documenting misconduct, warning humans or attempting to dissuade peers. I compare the same encounter across three conditions, separating notification use from response meaning and verified execution. Protection against a live swarm remains untested.
